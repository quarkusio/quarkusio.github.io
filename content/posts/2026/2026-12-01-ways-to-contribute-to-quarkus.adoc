When people think about contributing to an open source project, they usually think about pull requests.
Code matters, of course, but a project like Quarkus needs much more than code to stay healthy: it needs users who report problems, people who answer questions, testers for new versions, writers, translators, speakers, and data about how it is actually used.

The information about how to help is spread across many places: the contributing guide, the discussion page, the usage page, the working groups, the localized websites.
It took me a while to find all of them, one at a time.
This post puts them in one place, starting with the ones that take a few minutes and need no knowledge of the codebase.

== Say yes to build analytics

This is the easiest contribution of all, and you may already have been asked to make it.

Since Quarkus 3.2, the first time you start dev mode (`quarkus dev`, `mvn quarkus:dev`, etc.) Quarkus asks if you agree to share anonymous build data with the community.
If you say yes, each build sends a small set of anonymous information: the build type, the extensions you use, the Java version, the operating system, Maven or Gradle, the Quarkus version, whether you build a native executable, and your time zone as a rough idea of where Quarkus is used.
Source code, IP addresses and personal data are not collected.

Only extensions from known public group IDs (such as `io.quarkus` and `io.quarkiverse`) are reported, so the names of your company's internal extensions never leave your machine.

Why does it matter? Because without this data, the team has to guess which Java versions to support, which operating systems to test on, and which extensions deserve more attention.
In link:/blog/quarkus-insights-253-build-analytics/[Quarkus Insights #253], Bruno Baptista and Max Rydahl Andersen walked through a year of this data: Java version adoption, operating systems, the fastest growing extensions, and where in the world Quarkus is being built.
Every developer who said yes is part of that picture.

If you said no at the time, you can change your mind. The setting lives in `~/.redhat/io.quarkus.analytics.localconfig`:

[source,json]
----
{"disabled":false}
----

You can also enable or disable it for a single build with the `quarkus.analytics.disabled` system property.
All the details are on the link:/usage/[usage analytics page] and in the link:/usage/policy[usage policy].

== Tell the team how you use Quarkus

Analytics show numbers. Stories show context.

If you run Quarkus in production, the team wants to hear about it: what you built, why you picked Quarkus, what went well and what did not.
These stories are published as link:/userstories/[user stories] on this blog, and they help other teams decide whether Quarkus fits their needs.
Even a short conversation on one of the community channels below, explaining your use case, is useful input.

== Try new versions before they ship

New versions are published as pre-releases before the final release, and testing them is one of the most useful things a user can do.
Quarkus 4 is a good example: it went through several betas and candidate releases, and the link:/blog/quarkus-4-0-0-beta1-released/[Beta1 announcement] asked for exactly this help: end users testing their applications, and extension developers migrating their extensions.

You know your application better than anyone. If it breaks on a pre-release, that is a bug the team can fix before the final release instead of after it.
Watch the https://github.com/quarkusio/quarkus/releases[GitHub releases] for the next beta or candidate release, upgrade a branch of your project, run your tests, and report what you find.

If you want to go one step further, daily snapshots of the `main` branch are published as `999-SNAPSHOT` in the https://github.com/quarkusio/quarkus-ecosystem-ci/releases[quarkus-ecosystem-ci releases].
They are useful to check that a fix merged in `main` actually solves your problem, before it is released.
For CI pipelines, the https://github.com/quarkusio/install-quarkus-snapshots-action/[install-quarkus-snapshots-action] downloads and installs them for you.

If you maintain a Quarkiverse extension, the https://github.com/quarkusio/quarkus-ecosystem-ci[Quarkus Ecosystem CI] builds extensions against Quarkus, so breaking changes are caught before a release and not by your users.

== Report issues that can be fixed

A good bug report is a real contribution. A vague one costs maintainers time.

When you open an https://github.com/quarkusio/quarkus/issues[issue], the https://github.com/quarkusio/quarkus/blob/main/CONTRIBUTING.md#reporting-an-issue[contributing guide] asks for:

* a way to reproduce the problem,
* what you see and what you expected to see,
* your Quarkus, Java, Maven or Gradle, and GraalVM versions.

The most valuable thing you can attach is a small project that reproduces the problem.
It often turns hours of guessing into minutes of debugging.

== Join the conversation

Quarkus has several community channels, all listed on the link:/discussion/[discussion page]:

* https://quarkusio.zulipchat.com[Zulip], the community chat, where users, contributors and the core team talk every day.
* https://github.com/quarkusio/quarkus/discussions[GitHub Discussions], for longer conversations. The `design-discussions` category is where new features and working groups are discussed.
* The https://groups.google.com/d/forum/quarkus-dev[quarkus-dev mailing list].
* https://stackoverflow.com/questions/tagged/quarkus[Stack Overflow], with the `quarkus` tag, for usage questions.
* The Quarkus Community Call, every two weeks at 2PM Paris time. The calendar is on the link:/discussion/[discussion page], and recordings are on YouTube.
* link:/insights/[Quarkus Insights], the weekly show about what is happening in and around Quarkus.

You do not need a reason to join. Reading the conversations is the fastest way to learn where the project is going and where help is needed.

The link:/working-groups/[working groups] are worth a look too.
Each one has a clear goal, a project board and a list of issues, and the contributing guide says pull requests aligned with the working groups and the roadmap get priority in reviews.

== Help other users

Every question answered by the community is a question a maintainer does not have to answer.

You do not need to be an expert. If you solved a problem last month, you can help the next person who hits it on Stack Overflow, GitHub Discussions or Zulip.

Triage is another way to help: trying to reproduce a reported issue, asking for the missing versions, or pointing to a duplicate.
It is not glamorous, but it makes the work of maintainers much faster.

== Improve the docs, or translate them

Documentation is the front door of a project, and it is often the easiest place to start.
Found a typo, an outdated snippet or a missing step while following a guide? Fix it.

One thing to know: the guides are not edited in the website repository.
They live in the main repository, under https://github.com/quarkusio/quarkus/tree/main/docs/src/main/asciidoc[`docs/src/main/asciidoc`], so documentation pull requests go there.

The community also maintains localized versions of quarkus.io:

* https://github.com/quarkusio/ja.quarkus.io[ja.quarkus.io] for Japanese
* https://github.com/quarkusio/cn.quarkus.io[cn.quarkus.io] for simplified Chinese
* https://github.com/quarkusio/es.quarkus.io[es.quarkus.io] for Spanish
* https://github.com/quarkusio/pt.quarkus.io[pt.quarkus.io] for Brazilian Portuguese

Each repository explains how to contribute in its README.
If you want to start a translation for another language, open an issue in the https://github.com/quarkusio/quarkusio.github.io[website repository].

== Write code

If you want to contribute code, start with the https://github.com/quarkusio/quarkus/blob/main/CONTRIBUTING.md[contributing guide].
It covers the build, the coding conventions, and the rule that tests and documentation are not optional.

To find something to work on, look at the issues labeled https://github.com/quarkusio/quarkus/labels/good%20first%20issue[good first issue] and https://github.com/quarkusio/quarkus/labels/help%20wanted[help wanted], or at the issues linked to a working group.

Quarkus core is not the only option. https://github.com/quarkiverse[Quarkiverse] hosts hundreds of extensions, each with its own maintainers.
A smaller codebase and a smaller team can make the first contribution easier, and the extension you use every day might need exactly the fix you have in mind.

And yes, you can use AI tools.
Quarkus welcomes them, and the main repository even ships an https://github.com/quarkusio/quarkus/blob/main/AGENTS.md[`AGENTS.md`] file and a set of skills so coding agents follow the project's conventions.
The https://github.com/quarkusio/quarkus/blob/main/AI_POLICY.md[AI usage policy] asks for one thing in return: you stay the author.
Review what the tool produces, make sure you understand it, and be ready to explain your choices in the review, just as you would with code written by hand.
Used that way, AI helps you write better contributions, not just more of them.

== Share what you learn

Writing a blog post, recording a video, giving a talk at a meetup or a conference, running a workshop: all of these bring new people to Quarkus and help existing users.

You can also write on this blog. The https://github.com/quarkusio/quarkusio.github.io#writing-a-blog[website README] explains how: add yourself as an author, write your post, and open a pull request.

And sometimes the simplest thing is enough: share what you liked about Quarkus with your team, or follow and share the project on link:/socials/[social media].

== Support the foundation

Quarkus is part of the https://commonhaus.org[Commonhaus Foundation], a vendor neutral home for open source projects.
You can read more on the link:/foundation/[foundation page], see the link:/benefactors/[benefactors] who already support the project, and learn how you or your company can https://www.commonhaus.org/community/#support-our-efforts[support Commonhaus].

== Pick one

You do not have to do all of this. Pick one thing and do it this week:

* Say yes to link:/usage/[build analytics].
* Test your application with the next https://github.com/quarkusio/quarkus/releases[pre-release].
* Open a clear https://github.com/quarkusio/quarkus/issues[issue] with a reproducer.
* Join https://quarkusio.zulipchat.com[Zulip] or the next Community Call.
* Answer one question on https://stackoverflow.com/questions/tagged/quarkus[Stack Overflow].
* Fix one thing in the https://github.com/quarkusio/quarkus/tree/main/docs/src/main/asciidoc[docs], or translate one page.
* Pick a https://github.com/quarkusio/quarkus/labels/good%20first%20issue[good first issue].
* Write about what you built.

Every one of these makes Quarkus better. See you in the community!
